# SLOP1521 — Lecture Content Plan (all 12 weeks)
### Built on the existing labs and the original course design. All citations verified. Ready for the agent to draft slides from.

## Standing lecture structure (same shape every week)

1. **Introduction** — hook by capturing the audience's interest and attention with a story that raises an important question, problem, dilemma, brief context or background, one sentence limiting scope if necessary, a signpost of the body's key points, brief motivation to highlight importance.
2. **Definitions** — plain, deadpan definitions of this week's key terms, written as if for a reader with no prior exposure to them at all. This is deliberate: the joke is in defining "shower" with the same gravity as a real technical term, not in the definition being clever.
3. **Body** — 2–4 key points, each with the argument for why it matters, an example (drawn from the case studies established in the labs wherever possible), and a citation where real research applies. A **check-in question** is called out after each key point — something the lecturer asks the room directly.
4. **In-lecture activity** — one short, low-setup activity run inside the lecture itself, distinct from the full lab activity later in the week. Keeps every lecture interactive without duplicating lab content.
5. **Conclusion** — summary, significance (tie to thesis), confirmation the lecture met its aim, then a **coming up** line naming this week's lab and this week's reflection (due before next lecture). At the end, thanks + invitation for questions.
6. **References** — full list for the week.

**Slide rules throughout:** one idea per slide, short phrases and keywords over paragraphs, an image or chart wherever it helps, always captioned.

### Week 1 — Orientation
**Hook:** Three anonymised, real-sounding "exit survey" quotes from a fictional prior cohort — one who learned laundry from a video the night before moving out, one who's never had to introduce themselves to a stranger, one who thought "adulting" was a joke until it wasn't.
**Context:** A computer science degree teaches you to build reliable software. It rarely teaches you to run yourself just as reliably.
**Scope:** Diagnostic only today — no content taught, only baselined.
**Signpost:** How this course works · how it's assessed · the course's policies · your starting point.
**Motivation:** Every topic this semester predicts things that matter well beyond university.

**Definitions:**
- *Baseline* (noun): a starting measurement against which later change is compared.
- *Cohort* (noun): the group of students undertaking this course together, for statistical and moral-support purposes.
- *Habit* (noun): a regular behaviour or routine that you do automatically, often with little or no conscious thought
- *Schedule* (noun): a plan that lists activities, tasks, or events and the specific times they are meant to happen

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — How lectures, labs, and reflections fit together.** A dedicated slide walking through the standing weekly rhythm: the lecture introduces a topic and the real research behind it; the lab that same week puts it into practice with a hands-on activity; the reflection due before the following lecture asks you to apply it to your own week. Each piece does something the others don't — the lecture explains *why*, the lab gives you a *go at it*, the reflection makes it *personal and ongoing*. **Check-in question:** "If you only ever came to labs and skipped lectures, which part of this course would you be missing entirely?"
- **Key point 2 — How the course is assessed.** A full slide listing all five components and their weights: Weekly Reflections (15%), Assignment 1 — Makeover (15%), Assignment 2 — Touch Grass Field Study (20%), Assignment 3 — Time to Be an Adult (20%), Final Exam (30%). Follow with a one-slide teaser of the Assignment 3 HD-band criteria — the funniest artefact in the course, worth surfacing early. **Check-in question:** "Which single assessment is worth the most of your final grade?"
- **Key point 3 — Course staff, Course policies, summarised.** One slide briefly covering the staff members and their backgrounds. One slide covering: submissions are paper-based, handed to the convenor's office or a tutor, with email accepted only for extenuating circumstances; no laptops or screens in any lab, ever. **Check-in question:** "Under what circumstance is emailing your submission actually allowed?"
- **Key point 4 — Why these skills, and why now.** Example: the ten habit cards from the Week 1 lab. Citation: Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). *How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world.* European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. Average 66 days to form a habit (range 18–254). **Check-in question:** "Show of hands — who's tried to build a new habit in under a week and given up? That's what this study explains."
- **Key point 5 — Your starting point.** Hands off to the Week 1 lab's Starting Point Check.

**In-lecture activity:** Silent, anonymous show of hands: "Who has a regular laundry schedule? A regular bedtime? Has spoken to a stranger this week?" — no discussion, just a visible spread across the room, to be revisited in Week 12.

**Conclusion:** Summary — this course treats these skills as real, teachable, and assessed, and lectures, labs, and reflections each do distinct, complementary work. Significance — competence is a skill like any other; CS culture just never taught you this one. Aims met — you know how the course runs, how it's assessed, its policies, and where you're starting. **Coming up:** this week's lab (Triage and the Starting Point Check) and your first reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Lally et al. (2010).

### Week 2 — Personal Hygiene and Maintenance
**Hook:** The fridge — condiments only, one ageing lime — introduced here, reused in Weeks 4 and 10.
**Context:** Hygiene routines are usually taught once, in childhood, and never revisited.
**Scope:** Showering, hair washing, deodorant, laundry frequency, skincare only.
**Signpost:** Why frequency beats intention · a real maintenance calendar · what "good enough" looks like.
**Motivation:** The most immediately fixable topic in the course.

**Definitions:**
- *Shower* (noun): a fixture or enclosure in which a person stands to wash the body using a controlled spray of water, typically once per calendar day.
- *Deodorant* (noun): a substance applied to the underarms to reduce detectable odour, typically reapplied daily.
- *Laundry* (noun, uncountable): clothing and linens requiring washing — also, the act of washing them, ideally before the pile is visible from the doorway.
- *Haircut* (noun): a scheduled trim of head hair, recommended at an interval measured in weeks, not academic semesters.
- *Skincare* (noun): the practice of maintaining and improving the health, barrier function, and appearance of the skin.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Frequency, not intention.** Citation: Aiello, A. E., Coulborn, R. M., Perez, V., & Larson, E. L. (2008). *Effect of hand hygiene on infectious disease risk in the community setting: A meta-analysis.* American Journal of Public Health, 98(8), 1372–1381. Improved hand hygiene reduced gastrointestinal illness by 31% and respiratory illness by 21% across 30 pooled studies. **Check-in question:** "If washing your hands more often cuts your odds of getting sick by a fifth to a third, what does that suggest about the rest of this list?"
- **Key point 2 — The laundry pile as a schedule, not a personality trait.** Example: the sock pile, described for sourcing/generating a matching image. **Check-in question:** "Is your current laundry pile a schedule, or an event that happens to you?"
- **Key point 3 — What "good enough" looks like.** Previews Assignment 1's rubric.

**In-lecture activity:** Hand out (or project) a blank seven-day grid. Everyone fills in just one column — today's date — with what they actually did for hygiene today, no judgment, not shared aloud.

**Conclusion:** Summary — a schedule beats a feeling. Significance — cheapest topic to fix, and it compounds. Aims met — you have a real calendar template. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the Maintenance Calendar) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Aiello, Coulborn, Perez, & Larson (2008).

### Week 3 — Fashion Fundamentals
**Hook:** "Would you wear what you're wearing right now to a job interview?" — no need to answer aloud.
**Context:** Clothing measurably changes how a wearer thinks and acts, not just how they're perceived.
**Scope:** Occasion-matching and basic colour coordination only.
**Signpost:** Why clothing affects cognition · reading an occasion · assembling from what you own.
**Motivation:** The one topic with genuinely surprising research behind it.

**Definitions:**
- *Outfit* (noun): a coordinated set of garments selected for a specific occasion, as distinct from "whatever was on the floor."
- *Dress code* (noun): an unwritten or written expectation for attire at a given event, violation of which is noticed by everyone except the violator.
- *Occasion* (noun): an event with its own implicit clothing requirements, distinct from "any day that ends in Y."

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Clothing changes cognition, not just perception.** Citation: Adam, H., & Galinsky, A. D. (2012). *Enclothed cognition.* Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 48(4), 918–925. A coat described as a doctor's lab coat measurably improved sustained attention versus the same coat described as a painter's coat — the effect needs both the physical experience of wearing it and its symbolic meaning. **Check-in question:** "Name the two factors enclothed cognition depends on."
- **Key point 2 — Reading an occasion.** Example: the five outfit photos from the Week 3 lab, shown here with captions naming their best-fit occasion.
- **Key point 3 — Assembling from what you own.** Previews the lab's outfit-assembly task and Assignment 1.

**In-lecture activity:** Project two outfit photos side by side; class votes by show of hands which is interview-appropriate, then which is gym-appropriate — same two photos, different question, to show the answer depends on occasion, not the outfit alone.

**Conclusion:** Summary — what you wear changes how you perform, not just how you're seen. Significance — closest thing in the course to a genuine hack. Aims met — you can read an occasion and dress for it. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the outfit ranking exercise) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Adam & Galinsky (2012).

### Week 4 — Sleep, Health, and Exercise
**Hook:** Read Jordan's Week (Monday and Tuesday entries) aloud, straight-faced.
**Context:** Sleep, food, and exercise are the three areas of life most often "optimised in intention, never in practice."
**Scope:** Sleep duration and exercise frequency only; detailed nutrition science out of scope.
**Signpost:** How much sleep you actually need · a real plan vs. a hope · exercise as a frequency.
**Motivation:** The widest gap in the whole course between stated intention and actual behaviour.

**Definitions:**
- *Bedtime* (noun): a specific, named clock time at which one intends to be asleep, not merely "later."
- *Balanced meal* (noun): a plate containing more than one food group, particularly one that is not currently instant noodles.
- *Exercise* (noun): physical activity undertaken on a stated schedule, distinguished from "the walk to the vending machine."

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — How much sleep you actually need.** Citation: Hirshkowitz, M., Whiton, K., Albert, S. M., et al. (2015). *National Sleep Foundation's sleep time duration recommendations: methodology and results summary.* Sleep Health, 1(1), 40–43. Seven to nine hours recommended for adults 18–64. **Check-in question:** "Who got seven hours or more last night? Keep your hand up if you also got it the night before."
- **Key point 2 — Jordan's Week, fully unpacked.** Central worked example — six stated intentions, zero actual times.
- **Key point 3 — Exercise as a frequency.** Citation: Chekroud, S. R., Gueorguieva, R., Zheutlin, A. B., Paulus, M., Krumholz, H. M., Krystal, J. H., & Chekroud, A. M. (2018). *Association between physical exercise and mental health in 1.2 million individuals in the USA between 2011 and 2015.* The Lancet Psychiatry, 5(9), 739–746. Exercisers reported 1.49 fewer poor-mental-health days per month than non-exercisers; benefits peaked around 45 minutes, 3–5 times a week; team sports showed the largest association (22.3% fewer poor days); more than 3 hours a day was associated with worse outcomes than none at all. **Check-in question:** "According to this study, is more exercise always better? What does the data actually say?"

**In-lecture activity:** Pair-share: state your actual bedtime last night to your neighbour — no judgment, no follow-up questions allowed.

**Conclusion:** Summary — a plan has a time; an intention doesn't; more isn't always better. Significance — the topic most students believe they're managing and visibly aren't. Aims met — you have the real recommended range and know exercise has a sweet spot, not just a direction. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the sleep-and-exercise plan), this week's reflection, due before next lecture, and Assignment 1 due on Friday, 12:00. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Hirshkowitz et al. (2015); Chekroud et al. (2018).

### Week 5 — Touching Grass 101
**Hook:** State plainly: memory and attention performance improved by roughly 20% after an hour interacting with nature in a real study. Pause. No elaboration yet.
**Context:** "Go outside" has an actual, measured mechanism behind it.
**Scope:** Attention restoration only; general physical-health benefits out of scope.
**Signpost:** What happens to attention outdoors · why cities are worse for this specifically · what Thursday's lab will ask you to notice.
**Motivation:** Lowest content burden, highest immediate payoff in the course.

**Definitions:**
- *Outdoors* (noun): the region beyond a building's exterior walls, accessible via a door.
- *Sunlight* (noun): direct illumination from the sun, typically encountered before 2pm.
- *Fresh air* (noun): air that has not previously circulated through a shared bedroom for several consecutive days.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Attention restoration, defined plainly.** Citation: Berman, M. G., Jonides, J., & Kaplan, S. (2008). *The cognitive benefits of interacting with nature.* Psychological Science, 19(12), 1207–1212. Walking in a natural setting (or even viewing pictures of nature) improved directed-attention task performance versus a busy urban walk. **Check-in question:** "Would a walk through a busy street give you the same benefit as a walk through a park, according to this study?"
- **Key point 2 — Why this isn't about "fresh air."** Contrast a park walk against a street walk, same exercise, different attentional effect.
- **Key point 3 — What the lab will ask you to do.** Direct handoff to the Week 5 lab.

**In-lecture activity:** Ask everyone to look out the nearest window (or, if none, close their eyes and picture the last outdoor space they were in) for a few seconds of silence before continuing.

**Conclusion:** Summary — the outdoors measurably restores attention, not just mood. Significance — reframes vague advice as a specific, useful action. Aims met — you know the actual mechanism. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the outdoor field observation) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Berman, Jonides, & Kaplan (2008).

### Week 6 — Laptop and CS Separation
**Hook:** Everyone places their laptops and phone away right now — no comment needed.
**Context:** The mere presence of a phone, not its use, appears to measurably affect available attention.
**Scope:** Attention and presence effects only, not screen-time addiction broadly.
**Signpost:** What "brain drain" means here · why this isn't about willpower · what Thursday's lab replaces a screen with.
**Motivation:** Directly sets up the course's own no-laptops-in-labs policy.

**Definitions:**
- *Screen-free time* (noun): a scheduled period during which no screen is consulted, checked, or kept within reach "just in case."
- *Hobby* (noun): a recurring activity undertaken for enjoyment, not assessment, and not involving a compiler.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — The mere-presence effect.** Citation: Ward, A. F., Duke, K., Gneezy, A., & Bos, M. W. (2017). *Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity.* Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2(2), 140–154. Participants with phones on the desk (even face-down, even switched off) performed worse on memory and attention tasks than those with phones in another room; the effect scaled with how noticeable the phone was. **Check-in question:** "According to this study, does turning your phone off remove the effect?"
- **Key point 2 — Why this isn't a willpower failure.** A structural, measurable effect, not a personal failing — consistent with the course's own no-blame rule from Week 8's incident reports.
- **Key point 3 — What Thursday replaces a screen with.** Handoff to the Week 6 lab.

**In-lecture activity:** Referring back to prompt at the beginning, a quick, honest show of hands: who noticed an urge to check your phone, or to take out your laptop?

**Conclusion:** Summary — screens cost attention even unused. Significance — this is the mechanism behind the course's own policy. Aims met — you know why Thursday's lab has no screens. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the analogue brainstorm and game) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Ward, Duke, Gneezy, & Bos (2017).

### Week 7 — Small Talk and Silence
**Hook:** Two volunteers hold eye contact in silence for what feels like a long time — reveal it was about three seconds.
**Context:** There's a real, measured, average comfortable length for eye contact.
**Scope:** Eye contact and small-talk mechanics only.
**Signpost:** The actual comfortable length of eye contact · silence isn't a failure state · the small-talk formula.
**Motivation:** The most immediately practiceable topic in the course.

**Definitions:**
- *Small talk* (noun): brief, low-stakes conversation exchanged with someone who is not a close friend, typically covering weather, weekend plans, or transit delays.
- *Eye contact* (noun): the act of looking at another person's eyes during conversation, for a duration long enough to signal attention and short enough to avoid alarm.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — The actual comfortable length of eye contact.** Citation: Binetti, N., Harrison, C., Coutrot, A., Johnston, A., & Mareschal, I. (2016). *Pupil dilation as an index of preferred mutual gaze duration.* Royal Society Open Science, 3(7), 160086. Average preferred mutual gaze duration was around 3.2–3.3 seconds, consistent across a wide international sample. **Check-in question:** "What was the average preferred eye contact length in this study?"
- **Key point 2 — Silence is not a failure state.** Sets up the lab's deliberate silence exercise.
- **Key point 3 — The small-talk formula.** Name, one hobby, one recent trip.

**In-lecture activity:** In pairs with a neighbour, hold eye contact for a silently counted three seconds, then immediately use the small-talk formula once.

**Conclusion:** Summary — there's a real, short, comfortable length for eye contact, and silence isn't automatically awkward. Significance — de-mystifies two of the most-avoided parts of ordinary conversation. Aims met — you have a number and a formula. **Coming up:** this week's lab (small talk stations and reading the room) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Binetti et al. (2016).

### Week 8 — Friendship and Group Communication
**Hook:** Read aloud, verbatim: "I'm sorry you felt that way, but if you'd actually let me finish—" Ask what's wrong with it before explaining.
**Context:** Effective apologies have been studied directly.
**Scope:** Apologies and the incident-report format only.
**Signpost:** The six real components of an apology · which matter most · the incident-report format.
**Motivation:** The clearest, most directly actionable research in the course.

**Definitions:**
- *Apology* (noun): a statement acknowledging fault, distinct from a statement acknowledging that the other person has feelings.
- *Group chat* (noun): a shared messaging thread among several people, notable for periods of total silence followed by sudden, simultaneous activity.
- *Listening to someone* (verb): to give conscious attention to someone speaking, not while thinking about video games or your coding project

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — The six components of an effective apology.** Citation: Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., & Lount, R. B. (2016). *An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.* Negotiation and Conflict Management Research, 9(2), 177–196. Six components: expression of regret, explanation, acknowledgment of responsibility, declaration of repentance, offer of repair, request for forgiveness. **Check-in question:** "Which component does 'I'm sorry you felt that way, but—' completely skip?"
- **Key point 2 — Which components matter most.** Per the same study, acknowledgment of responsibility mattered most, then offer of repair; request for forgiveness mattered least.
- **Key point 3 — The incident-report format.** Reuses the Week 8 lab's format.

**In-lecture activity:** Split the room in half. One half drafts a bad apology (missing acknowledgment of responsibility on purpose); the other drafts a fix. Read two or three aloud.

**Conclusion:** Summary — a good apology has real, identifiable components, and some matter more than others. Significance — turns "be a better friend" into a checklist. Aims met — you can name what a bad apology is missing. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the ghosted-reply role-play and incident report), this week's reflection, due before next lecture, and Assignment 2 due on Friday, 12:00. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Lewicki, Polin, & Lount (2016).

### Week 9 — Dating Without Documentation
*(Lighter touch, as established — no personal disclosure required.)*
**Hook:** Read the first two sentences of the 600-word message, then ask the room to guess the total length. Reveal: 600 words.
**Context:** People reliably overestimate how clearly their tone comes across in text.
**Scope:** Text-based tone misjudgement only.
**Signpost:** Why text is worse at carrying tone · what the 600 words were doing · reading between the lines.
**Motivation:** Explains why overthinking a message doesn't make it clearer.

**Definitions:**
- *Asking someone out* (verb phrase): directly proposing a specific, named activity, at a specific, named time, to a specific person — as distinct from implying it at length.
- *Text message* (noun): a short written message sent electronically, conventionally understood to convey less tone than its author believes.
- *Flirting* (verb): to show superficial or casual interest or liking to another person, without providing an essay about your interest in Linux while the other person is entirely uninterested

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Why text is bad at carrying tone.** Citation: Kruger, J., Epley, N., Parker, J., & Ng, Z. (2005). *Egocentrism over e-mail: Can we communicate as well as we think?* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 89(6), 925–936. Senders expected about 78% accuracy in how their intended tone would be read over text; actual accuracy was no better than chance. **Check-in question:** "How much better than a coin flip were readers at correctly guessing tone over text, in this study?"
- **Key point 2 — What the 600 words were actually doing.** Identify the repeated hedges and self-interruptions, tie to the overconfidence finding above.
- **Key point 3 — Reading between the lines.** Handoff to the lab's ambiguous-message exercise.

**In-lecture activity (opt-in):** Project one short, genuinely ambiguous text (e.g. "lol" with nothing else) and take a quick, anonymous poll: sincere or dismissive? Reveal the split — usually close to even, which is the point.

**Conclusion:** Summary — more words rarely means clearer tone. Significance — reframes overthinking a message as measurable and common, not a personal flaw. Aims met — you know why the 600 words didn't help. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the 600-word message and reading between the lines) and this week's reflection — optional, as always for this week. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Kruger, Epley, Parker, & Ng (2005).

### Week 10 — Basic Adulting 101
**Hook:** The fridge's third and final appearance — price out what's in it versus what a week of groceries should cost.
**Context:** Budgeting failure is rarely about income — usually about the absence of named categories.
**Scope:** Basic budget categorisation and grocery planning only.
**Signpost:** Why naming categories matters · a real grocery list · what Assignment 3's budget section wants.
**Motivation:** The most transferable topic beyond graduation.

**Definitions:**
- *Budget* (noun): a named allocation of money to specific categories, prepared in advance of spending, not reconstructed afterward from bank statements.
- *Grocery list* (noun): a written record of food to purchase, prepared before entering a supermarket, not while standing in it.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Why naming categories matters.** Citation: Warren, E., & Warren Tyagi, A. (2005). *All Your Worth: The Ultimate Lifetime Money Plan.* Free Press. The 50/30/20 rule: 50% of after-tax income to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings/debt — three named categories rather than an unstructured sense of caution. **Check-in question:** "If your weekly budget is $200, what does 50/30/20 look like in actual dollars?"
- **Key point 2 — The fridge, priced out.** Contrast the fridge's contents against a real, planned weekly grocery list.
- **Key point 3 — What Assignment 3 actually wants.** Preview the budget component of the HD-band criteria.

**In-lecture activity:** In pairs, apply 50/30/20 to a fixed hypothetical weekly amount (e.g. $150) and name one specific thing in each of the three buckets.

**Conclusion:** Summary — categories, not totals, make a budget work. Significance — clearest lifetime payoff of any topic here. Aims met — you have a real, priced grocery list template and a named-category method. **Coming up:** this week's lab (budget triage and the grocery list) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Warren & Warren Tyagi (2005).

### Week 11 — Workplace Behaviour
**Hook:** Read the full bad email from the Week 11 lab aloud, flat and professional, no commentary.
**Context:** Interviews and emails are both skills with real, measured best practices.
**Scope:** Structured interview answers and email brevity only.
**Signpost:** Why structured answers beat instinct · why the bad email fails · the three-sentence rule.
**Motivation:** Most directly tied to life immediately after this course ends.

**Definitions:**
- *Interview* (noun): a structured conversation in which a candidate's suitability for a role is assessed via prepared questions, not vibes.
- *Professional email* (noun): a written workplace message with one clearly stated purpose, ideally identifiable without reading past the second sentence.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Structured answers beat improvised ones.** Citation: Schmidt, F. L., & Hunter, J. E. (1998). *The validity and utility of selection methods in personnel psychology: Practical and theoretical implications of 85 years of research findings.* Psychological Bulletin, 124(2), 262–274. Structured interviews showed a predictive validity of about .51 for job performance, meaningfully higher than unstructured interviews; combined with a general mental ability measure, validity rose to about .63. **Check-in question:** "According to this meta-analysis, is a free-flowing, unstructured interview actually a better predictor of job performance, or worse?"
- **Key point 2 — Why the bad email fails.** Line-by-line: buried ask, rambling opening, an irrelevant tangent — connect back to Week 9's Kruger et al. (2005) finding on egocentrism in text.
- **Key point 3 — The three-sentence rule.** Handoff to the lab's shrink-down exercise.

**In-lecture activity:** Cold-call for a volunteer; ask them one real interview question live ("tell us about yourself") and time the answer — then discuss as a class what made it structured or rambling.

**Conclusion:** Summary — both interviews and emails reward structure over instinct. Significance — most immediate real-world stakes of any topic in the course. Aims met — you have a structure for interview answers and a length target for emails. **Coming up:** this week's lab (the mock interview and the shrink-down) and this week's reflection, due before next lecture. Thanks + questions.

**References:** Schmidt & Hunter (1998); Kruger, Epley, Parker, & Ng (2005) (callback).

### Week 12 — Integration
**Hook:** Return to Week 1's empty chart — fill it in live with the class's anonymised, aggregated self-reported change.
**Context:** This week teaches nothing new — it's designed to prove retention, not add content.
**Scope:** Full-semester review, structured as evidence-gathering.
**Signpost:** Revisiting Week 1's habit-formation research · what actually changed and how we know · closing the loop.
**Motivation:** The course's central claim gets checked here, not just asserted.

**Definitions:**
- *Evidence* (noun, callback): something that actually happened. Contrast with *intention* (noun): something you meant to do.

**Body:**
- **Key point 1 — Revisiting Lally et al. (2010).** A 12-week semester falls short of the 66-day average from Week 1 — stated honestly, not overclaimed. **Check-in question:** "Based on Week 1's research, would you expect every habit in this course to be fully formed by today?"
- **Key point 2 — What actually changed, and how we know.** Reuses the Then and Now lab method: evidence, not intention.
- **Key point 3 — Closing the loop.** A final callback to every running example: the fridge, the sock pile, Jordan's Week, the 600-word message.

**In-lecture activity:** Repeat Week 1's anonymous show-of-hands questions verbatim (laundry schedule, bedtime, spoken to a stranger this week) and compare the spread live against Week 1's.

**Conclusion:** Summary — real, evidenced change, however partial, over the exact timeframe the first lecture's own research predicted. Significance — read the thesis line aloud once, without further comment. Aims met — the semester's promise checked against evidence, not just claimed. **Coming up:** the final lab (Then and Now, and the capstone showcase) — no further reflection due, and Assignment 3 due on Friday, 12:00. Thanks + questions — and thanks for the semester.

**References:** Lally et al. (2010) (callback).
