# SLOP1521 — In-Lab Activities (all 12 weeks)
### Source document for content generation — fully specified, ready to use directly

## Standing rules for every lab

- **No laptops or screens in any lab, ever** — this is a stated course policy, not just a Week 6 topic. It matches the course's own philosophy, and it should be mentioned once, plainly, on the labs overview page.
- **No technical/CS jargon anywhere in this content** — not as a metaphor, not as a pun, not as flavour. Plain, everyday language throughout.
- **Maximise the stereotypes, not the cleverness.** Every example below should be as specific and recognisable as possible — the humour comes from precision, not from being clever about computing.
- **Same five-block shape every week** (below), so only the content changes, never the structure.

| Time | Block |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | Tutor framing — this week's topic, tie back to last week's reflection |
| 0:05–0:20 | Activity 1 (usually groups of 3–4) |
| 0:20–0:30 | Presentation / class discussion of Activity 1 |
| 0:30–0:45 | Activity 2 (usually pairs) |
| 0:45–0:55 | Class discussion / debrief of Activity 2 |
| 0:55–1:00 | Wrap-up, look-ahead, reflection prompt reminder |

### Week 1 — Orientation
**Activity 1 (groups of 4): Triage.**
Each group gets these ten cards (print each on its own card):
1. Has reused the same tea bag three times this week.
2. Bedsheets have not been changed since move-in day.
3. Eats standing at the fridge more often than sitting at a table.
4. Has not spoken to a stranger, unprompted, since enrolment.
5. Owns seven identical hoodies and considers this a wardrobe.
6. Says "I showered this morning" — three mornings in a row.
7. Has a chair that has become permanent storage for clean laundry.
8. Replies to messages exclusively between midnight and 3am.
9. Has eaten the same meal five nights running and calls it a routine.
10. Has not seen sunlight before 2pm, as far as they can recall.

Groups sort the ten into Critical / Monitor / Stable and must justify their top and bottom pick.

**Discussion:** Compare lists across groups — disagreement is the point; it previews that "how bad is this, really" is exactly what the semester will pin down properly.

**Activity 2 (pairs): Starting point check.**
Partners compare their (private) Week 1 self-assessment scores at a category level only — no specifics required — and each names one thing they expect to improve fastest.

**Wrap-up:** Frame the semester as a starting point, not a judgement.

### Week 2 — Personal Hygiene and Maintenance
**Activity 1 (groups of 3): Maintenance calendar.**
Each group drafts a shared weekly calendar template (a real seven-day grid) covering: shower, hair wash, laundry, and one "haircut due" reminder column. Balance realistic frequency against plausible first-year effort.

**Presentation:** One group's calendar goes on the board; the class critiques the frequencies chosen.

**Activity 2 (pairs): Peer check.**
Each person fills in their own real calendar privately, then swaps with their partner for one honest, one-line comment only — no further detail required.

**Wrap-up:** Calendars get referenced again in the Week 4 reflection.

### Week 3 — Fashion Fundamentals
**Activity 1 (groups of 4): Ranking exercise.**
Each group gets five outfit photos (source or generate stock images matching these descriptions):
1. Business-casual — collared shirt, chinos, clean sneakers.
2. Head-to-toe loungewear — hoodie, tracksuit pants, slides.
3. Overdressed — a full suit with a novelty tie.
4. Gym kit — athletic shorts, graphic t-shirt, running shoes.
5. Slept-in look — creased shirt, visibly mismatched socks, uncombed hair.

Each group ranks the five for one assigned occasion (lecture, first date, job interview, gym, or a funeral), then groups rotate occasions and re-rank.

**Presentation:** Each group presents their ranking and the one outfit they were least sure about.

**Activity 2 (individual, shared in pairs): Assemble three outfits** — one for a lecture, one for a friend's birthday, one for the gym — from clothes you already own, justified in one sentence each. This is a rehearsal for Assignment 1, not the submission. Partners each offer one suggestion.

**Wrap-up:** Note which occasion the class ranked least confidently.

### Week 4 — Sleep and Health
**Activity 1 (groups of 4): Case study.**
Hand out this case study:

> **Jordan's Week**
> Monday: Plan says asleep by 11:30pm. Actual: still awake at 3am finishing an assignment. Plan says gym at 7am. Actual: alarm snoozed four times, gym skipped.
> Tuesday: "Will sleep early tonight," typed into a note that is never looked at again. Stayed up until 2am watching one more episode. Exercise logged as "30 min run"; actually walked to the vending machine and back twice.
> Wednesday: Skipped breakfast, ate two-minute noodles at 1pm and again at 9pm. Told a friend "I've been so good with food lately."
> Thursday: Went to bed on time for the first time all week. Woke up anyway at 4am to check a phone notification and stayed up.
> Friday: No plan was made for this day at all.
> Saturday: Slept until 2pm to "catch up," then couldn't sleep until 4am that night.
> Sunday: Wrote next week's plan. It is identical to this week's plan.
>
> Jordan's week contains six stated intentions and zero actual times.

Groups analyse: what's realistic, what's just wishful thinking, what will fail first, and the single most useful fix.

**Presentation:** Each group presents their one best fix only.

**Activity 2 (individual, checked in pairs): Build your own plan** — a target bedtime and a stated fallback for the night it slips, and an exercise plan with a stated frequency and a named activity. Partners reject any plan that reads as "sleep more" or "exercise more" rather than a real time.

**Wrap-up:** Plans are logged and referenced again in Week 12.

### Week 5 — Touching Grass 101
**Activity 1 (pairs, outdoors, 15 min): Field observation.**
Leave the room. Record three specific, non-screen observations: one thing seen, one thing heard, and one brief interaction with another person, however small.

**Return, discussion:** Share one observation each.

**Activity 2 (groups of 4): Field guide.**
Groups compile the class's observations into a short "field guide to outside" — three tips for someone who has never been outdoors before.

**Wrap-up:** This week is assessed on attendance outdoors, not on written quality.

### Week 6 — Laptop and CS Separation
**Activity 1 (groups of 4, paper only): Brainstorm.**
Mind-map one hobby or interest that has nothing to do with computers or gaming. The group picks the most promising one to develop further.

**Presentation:** Each group pitches their hobby in 30 seconds. No technical language allowed — the tutor calls it out live if it slips in.

**Activity 2 (pairs): Pick a game.**
Choose one from this list, or bring an equivalent: a deck of cards (for a simple game like Go Fish or a round of Uno), a small jigsaw puzzle, dominoes, checkers, a physical crossword, or Jenga. Play for the full block.

**Wrap-up:** Ask who reached for their phone first, and how many minutes in.

### Week 7 — Small Talk and Silence
**Activity 1 (rotating pairs, 15 min): Small talk stations.**
Two-minute rounds, new partner each round. Before each round, sit in silence for 30 seconds first — deliberately, no talking — then use the prompt: ask their name, one hobby, and their most recent trip somewhere. Rotate three times.

**Discussion:** Which felt harder — the silence, or the talking?

**Activity 2 (groups of 4): Reading the room.**
One "actor" per group performs a short, ambiguous social cue (checking their phone repeatedly, giving one-word answers, looking at the door). The rest of the group has 60 seconds to name what's happening and suggest a graceful way to end the conversation.

**Wrap-up:** Collect the best exit lines on the board.

### Week 8 — Friendship and Group Communication
**Activity 1 (groups of 4): Role-play — the unanswered message.**
One student plays a friend who sent a reasonable message three days ago and got no reply. Another plays the person who left them on read. The rest of the group watches and calls out the exact moment an apology goes wrong (e.g. "I'm sorry you felt that way, but—").

**Discussion:** What's the earliest point the apology could have been saved?

**Activity 2 (pairs): Incident report.**
Write up a real or invented mix-up between friends as a short report: what was agreed, what actually happened, and what actually went wrong and why — traced to a specific chat message or a specific missed step, never to anyone's character. Partners review each other's "what went wrong" line, since that's the hardest part to get right without blaming a person.

**Wrap-up:** Best example goes up as this week's model answer.

### Week 9 — Dating Without Documentation
*(Lighter touch, as noted elsewhere: role-play here uses the fictional character below, not personal disclosure. Taking a speaking role is optional — an observer role is always available.)*

**Activity 1 (groups of 4, opt-in roles): The 600-word message.**
One student reads aloud the message below; the group edits it, live, down to one sentence that says the same thing.

> hey!! okay so this is going to sound weird and you can totally just ignore this if it's weird, no pressure at all, but I've been meaning to say hi for a while now and I just never really found the right moment, like there's never really a "right moment" is there, everyone always talks about finding the right moment but I think that's kind of made up if I'm honest, anyway that's not the point, the point is I remember you mentioned in that one lecture (I think it was week 3? or maybe week 4, I genuinely can't remember, my memory for weeks is terrible but my memory for what people say is actually really good, which is a weird combination I know) that you liked [hobby], and I thought that was really cool because I've always wanted to try that but never had the guts to, and I don't know, I guess I just wanted to say that was cool, and also maybe ask if you wanted to get coffee sometime, no worries if not though, genuinely no pressure, I'm not going to be weird about it either way, we can just pretend this never happened if you'd rather, actually wait, ignore that, that makes it sound like I want you to say no, I don't want you to say no, but I also don't want you to feel like you HAVE to say yes, anyway I'm going to stop typing now because I think this message is getting long, sorry, I'll stop, okay bye, unless you want to say something, in which case, hi.

**Discussion:** What did the rest of those words actually accomplish?

**Activity 2 (pairs): Reading between the lines.**
Given each short exchange below, discuss what the other person probably means, and draft one honest, low-stakes reply:
1. They reply "lol" to something you thought was genuinely funny — and nothing else.
2. They say "we should hang out sometime" with no day or time attached.
3. A message starts typing, stops, and nothing arrives for ten minutes.

**Wrap-up:** This week's reflection stays optional, as already established.

### Week 10 — Basic Adulting 101
**Activity 1 (groups of 4): Budget triage.**
Given a fixed weekly budget and these categories — groceries, transport, subscriptions, "emergency" takeaway, savings — groups must cut 20% from the total and decide where it comes from.

**Presentation:** Each group shares their most controversial cut.

**Activity 2 (pairs): Grocery list.**
Using this week's meal-plan brief, draft a real one-week grocery list that repeats no meal more than twice and keeps instant noodles under the stated limit.

**Wrap-up:** Lists feed directly into Assignment 3.

### Week 11 — Workplace Behaviour
**Activity 1 (pairs): Mock interview.**
Ask your partner these questions, in order, then swap:
1. Tell us about yourself.
2. Describe a time you disagreed with someone on a group project. What happened?
3. Why does this role interest you?
4. What's something you're still working on improving?
5. Where do you want to be in a few years?

Feedback is limited to one thing that worked and one specific fix — not a general critique.

**Activity 2 (groups of 4): The shrink-down.**
Each group gets this email and has to cut it to three sentences without losing the actual request:

> Hi team,
>
> Hope you're all doing well! Just wanted to reach out and touch base about a few things. So I've been thinking a lot about the project timeline, and I know we all discussed this a bit last week, but I wanted to circle back and get everyone's thoughts again since things have kind of shifted a little since then. There's a lot going on this week for me personally so I might be a bit slow to respond to things, just a heads up. Anyway, the main thing is I think we might need an extra day for the deadline, or maybe two, not totally sure yet, still figuring that out. Let me know what you all think when you get a chance, no rush though! Also random thought but should we get lunch sometime this week? Anyway let me know about the deadline thing.
>
> Thanks so much,
> [Name]

Shortest version that keeps the actual ask (more time is needed, roughly how much, and a request for a response) wins.

**Wrap-up:** Winning version goes on the board.

---

### Week 12 — Integration
**Activity 1 (individual, then pairs): Then and now.**
Compare your Week 1 self-assessment to today. Pick the one thing that's changed the most, and back it up with evidence — something you actually did, not just an intention.

**Activity 2 (groups of 4): Showcase.**
Each group presents one member's biggest change as a short case study, in the same shape as Week 8's incident report — but this time, the outcome is good.

**Wrap-up:** Close the semester by reading the course's opening line aloud once, without further comment.
