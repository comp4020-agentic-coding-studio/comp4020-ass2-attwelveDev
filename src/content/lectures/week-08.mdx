## Introduction

"I'm sorry you felt that way, but if you'd actually let me finish—" — read
aloud, verbatim. What's wrong with it, before we explain it? Effective
apologies have been studied directly; today's scope is listening,
apologies, and the incident-report format only, the clearest, most
directly actionable research in the course.

## Body

- **Actually listening, not waiting to talk.** Put the phone face-down
  and stop composing your reply while the other person is still
  speaking. Before you respond, reflect back one thing they actually
  said — "so you're saying X" — then add your own point. If you can't
  repeat back what they just said, you weren't listening, you were
  waiting for your turn.

<CheckIn question="Could you repeat back the last full sentence your neighbour said before this lecture started?" />

- **The six components of an effective apology.** Lewicki, R. J., Polin,
  B., & Lount, R. B. (2016) name six: expression of regret, explanation,
  acknowledgment of responsibility, declaration of repentance, offer of
  repair, request for forgiveness.

<CheckIn question="Which component does 'I'm sorry you felt that way, but—' completely skip?" />

- **Which components matter most.** Per the same study, acknowledgment
  of responsibility mattered most, then offer of repair; request for
  forgiveness mattered least.

<CheckIn question="If you only had time for one component, which one does the research say to keep?" />

- **All six, assembled into one apology.** "I should have told you the
  plan changed instead of letting you find out from someone else
  [acknowledgment of responsibility]. I got caught up finalising the venue
  and didn't think to message you until it was already too late
  [explanation]. I'm sorry — that's on me, not a good excuse
  [expression of regret]. I'll message you directly next time, not just
  the group chat [declaration of repentance]. I'd like to shout you
  coffee before the next one [offer of repair]." No request for
  forgiveness — it mattered least, and this version doesn't need one.

<CheckIn question="Which of the six components is missing from this apology on purpose, and why does the study say that's fine?" />

- **The incident-report format.** What was agreed, what actually
  happened, and the actual reason — traced to a process failure (a
  message routed to the wrong chat, a step nobody owned) rather than to
  any one person's character. The same no-blame reading this course
  applies everywhere else.

<CheckIn question="Why does the format ask for a process failure specifically, instead of just asking 'whose fault was it'?" />

## Conclusion

A good apology has real, identifiable components, and some matter more
than others — this turns "be a better friend" into a checklist. You can
now tell the difference between listening and waiting for your turn,
name exactly what a bad apology is missing, assemble a real one, and
write up a mix-up as an incident report that traces the actual reason
instead of assigning blame. Coming up: this week's lab (the ghosted-reply
