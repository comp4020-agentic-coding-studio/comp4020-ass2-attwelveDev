## Introduction

The opening of a real message, recovered from a phone's drafts folder,
typed in one unbroken forty-minute sitting and never sent: "hey!! okay
so this is going to sound weird and you can totally just ignore this if
it's weird, no pressure at all—" read aloud, then stop. Now guess the
total length. It was six hundred words. People reliably overestimate how
clearly their tone comes across in text; today's scope is text-based
tone misjudgement, reading and writing an answerable message, and
flirting only, which explains why overthinking a message doesn't make
it any clearer.

## Body

- **Why text is bad at carrying tone.** Kruger, J., Epley, N., Parker,
  J., & Ng, Z. (2005) found senders expected about 78% accuracy in how
  their intended tone would be read over text; actual accuracy was no
  better than chance.

<CheckIn question="How much better than a coin flip were readers at correctly guessing tone over text, in this study?" />

- **The six hundred words, in full.** Recovered word for word from a
  phone's drafts folder, where it sat for six days after being typed in a
  single unbroken forty-minute sitting and never sent. Reproduced exactly
  as written, out of academic necessity, with no edits of any kind:

  > hey!! okay so this is going to sound weird and you can totally just
  > ignore this if it's weird, no pressure at all, but I've been meaning to
  > say hi for a while now and I just never really found the right moment,
  > like there's never really a "right moment" is there, everyone always
  > talks about finding the right moment but I think that's kind of made up
  > if I'm honest, anyway that's not the point, the point is I remember you
  > mentioned in that one lecture (I think it was week 3? or maybe week 4, I
  > genuinely can't remember, my memory for weeks is terrible but my memory
  > for what people say is actually really good, which is a weird
  > combination I know) that you liked urban beekeeping, and I thought that was
  > really cool because I've always wanted to try that but never had the
  > guts to, and I don't know, I guess I just wanted to say that was cool,
  > and also maybe ask if you wanted to get coffee sometime, no worries if
  > not though, genuinely no pressure, I'm not going to be weird about it
  > either way, we can just pretend this never happened if you'd rather,
  > actually wait, ignore that, that makes it sound like I want you to say
  > no, I don't want you to say no, but I also don't want you to feel like
  > you HAVE to say yes, anyway I'm going to stop typing now because I think
  > this message is getting long, sorry, I'll stop, okay bye, unless you
  > want to say something, in which case, hi.

  A shared class, three hobbies, a five-year plan, and a joke needing
  context the reader didn't have — repeated hedges and self-interruptions,
  all consistent with a sender overestimating how clearly the words alone
  were landing.

<CheckIn question="What did all the words after 'I thought that was really cool' actually add?" />

- **Reading between the lines, and cutting your own.** For someone else's
  ambiguous message, the same technique from Week 7 applies: diagnose
  what's actually being said before responding to the words on the
  surface. For your own messages, run the edit in reverse: cut everything
  that isn't the one question you actually want answered, then check that
  what's left is still answerable with a yes, a no, or a time. If it
  can't be answered in one line, it isn't one question yet.

<CheckIn question="Which single sentence in the 600-word message is the one actual question?" />

- **Flirting doesn't require decoding.** State the specific thing you
  noticed and the specific thing you're proposing — not an implication
  the other person has to interpret. "I liked that you mentioned urban
  beekeeping — want to get coffee sometime?" survives being read literally;
  six hundred words of hedging doesn't.

## Conclusion

More words rarely means clearer tone — this reframes overthinking a
message as measurable and common, not a personal flaw. You now know why
six hundred words didn't help, and how to cut your own message down to
the one question inside it. Coming up: this week's lab (the 600-word
message and reading between the lines).
