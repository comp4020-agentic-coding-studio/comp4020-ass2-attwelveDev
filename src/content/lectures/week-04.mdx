## Introduction

Monday: plan says asleep by 11:30pm, actual is still awake at 3am
finishing an assignment. Tuesday: "will sleep early tonight," typed into a
note never looked at again. Sleep, food, and exercise are the three areas
of life most often optimised in intention, never in practice — today's
scope is sleep duration, exercise frequency, and what actually counts as
a balanced meal, plus the fridge from Week 2, revisited; detailed
nutrition science (macronutrient targets, calorie counts) stays out of
scope.

## Body

- **How much sleep you actually need, and how to get there.**
  Hirshkowitz, M., Whiton, K., Albert, S. M., et al. (2015) recommend
  seven to nine hours for adults 18–64 — a real range, not a rough guess.
  Two things get you there: a wake time fixed to the same clock time
  every day, weekends included, and a screen wind-down that starts at a
  named time before bed, not "whenever I feel tired."

- **Jordan's Week, fully unpacked.** Monday: asleep-by-11:30 plan, awake
  at 3am finishing an assignment — the 7am gym slot goes the same way,
  four snoozes then skipped entirely. Tuesday: "will sleep early
  tonight," written down and never reopened; 2am arrives via one more
  episode, and the run logged in the plan was a walk to the vending
  machine and back, twice. Wednesday: breakfast skipped, the same bowl
  of instant noodles twice in one day, reported to a friend, straight-
  faced, as "I've been so good with food lately." Thursday: bed on time
  for once, then up at 4am for a single phone notification. Friday: no
  plan was written at all. Saturday: sleep until 2pm to "catch up,"
  followed by lying awake until 4am that night. Sunday: next week's plan,
  copied from this one, unchanged. Six stated intentions, zero actual
  times — the week's own Lab breaks down exactly where each plan met
  reality.

- **Exercise as a frequency, not a location.** Chekroud, S. R.,
  Gueorguieva, R., Zheutlin, A. B., Paulus, M., Krumholz, H. M., Krystal,
  J. H., & Chekroud, A. M. (2018) found exercisers reported 1.49 fewer
  poor-mental-health days per month than non-exercisers, peaking around
  45 minutes 3–5 times a week — but more than 3 hours a day was
  associated with worse outcomes than none at all. A beginner routine
  that hits that range: three sessions a week, 20–30 minutes each, at a
  stated time slot — any activity that raises your heart rate counts, and
  a gym session is one option, not the only one; a brisk walk on a fixed
  route satisfies the same frequency.

<CheckIn question="According to this study, is more exercise always better? What does the data actually say?" />

- **The fridge, revisited.** Week 2's fridge shelf hasn't changed:
  condiments, one ageing lime, still nothing that constitutes a meal —
  which is exactly why Wednesday's plan in Jordan's Week collapses into
  the same bowl of instant noodles twice in a day. A sleep schedule and
  an exercise plan both assume there's something to eat between them;
  an empty fridge breaks both.

<CheckIn question="What's the direct link between an empty fridge and Wednesday's two servings of instant noodles?" />

- **How to actually build a balanced meal.** Three items on the plate,
  at the same sitting, not spread across the day: a base (rice, pasta,
  bread), a protein (eggs, canned beans, last night's leftover chicken),
  and something that isn't beige (a vegetable — any vegetable, frozen
  counts). A piece of fruit eaten four hours after the noodles doesn't
  retroactively balance the noodles. The only way the fridge from Week 2
  supplies all three on a Wednesday is if it was stocked on the weekend
  before, not raided the night of.

## Conclusion

A plan has a time; an intention doesn't. More isn't always better. This is
the topic most students believe they're managing and visibly aren't — you
now have the real recommended range, a fixed wake time and wind-down to
get there, a beginner exercise routine that isn't tied to any one
location, the fridge as a reminder that a schedule needs something to
actually run on, and the three-item test for whether what's on the plate
actually counts as a meal. Coming up: this week's lab (the
