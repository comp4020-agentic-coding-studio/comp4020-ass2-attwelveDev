## Definitions

- **Asking someone out** (verb phrase) — directly proposing a specific, named activity, at a specific, named time, to a specific person — as distinct from implying it at length.
- **Text message** (noun) — a short written message sent electronically, conventionally understood to convey less tone than its author believes.
- **Flirting** (verb) — to show superficial or casual interest or liking to another person, without providing an essay about your interests while the other person is entirely uninterested.

## Key point 1 — Why text is bad at carrying tone

Kruger, J., Epley, N., Parker, J., & Ng, Z. (2005). *Egocentrism over e-mail: Can we communicate as well as we think?* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 89(6), 925–936.

Senders expected about 78% accuracy in how their intended tone would be read over text.

Actual accuracy was no better than chance.

## Key point 2 — The 600 words, part one

Recovered word for word from a phone's drafts folder, where it sat for six days after being typed in a single unbroken forty-minute sitting and never sent.

> hey!! okay so this is going to sound weird and you can totally just ignore this if it's weird, no pressure at all, but I've been meaning to say hi for a while now and I just never really found the right moment, like there's never really a "right moment" is there, everyone always talks about finding the right moment but I think that's kind of made up if I'm honest, anyway that's not the point, the point is I remember you mentioned in that one lecture (I think it was week 3? or maybe week 4, I genuinely can't remember, my memory for weeks is terrible but my memory for what people say is actually really good, which is a weird combination I know) that you liked urban beekeeping,

```notes
Read it at a normal pace, straight-faced. The length is the joke; don't undersell it by rushing.
```

## Key point 3 — The 600 words, part two

> and I thought that was really cool because I've always wanted to try that but never had the guts to, and I don't know, I guess I just wanted to say that was cool, and also maybe ask if you wanted to get coffee sometime, no worries if not though, genuinely no pressure, I'm not going to be weird about it either way, we can just pretend this never happened if you'd rather, actually wait, ignore that, that makes it sound like I want you to say no,

---

## Key point 4 — The 600 words, part three

> I don't want you to say no, but I also don't want you to feel like you HAVE to say yes, anyway I'm going to stop typing now because I think this message is getting long, sorry, I'll stop, okay bye, unless you want to say something, in which case, hi.

---

## Key point 5 — What it was actually doing

A shared class, three hobbies, a five-year plan, and a joke needing context the reader didn't have.

Repeated hedges and self-interruptions — consistent with overestimating how clearly the words alone were landing.

<CheckIn question="What did all the words after 'I thought that was really cool' actually add?" />

## Key point 6 — Reading between the lines, and cutting your own

For someone else's ambiguous message: diagnose what's actually being said before responding to the words on the surface — the same technique from Week 7.

For your own message: cut everything that isn't the one question you actually want answered.

If it can't be answered in one line, it isn't one question yet.

<CheckIn question="Which single sentence in the 600-word message is the one actual question?" />

---

## Key point 7 — Flirting doesn't require decoding

State the specific thing you noticed and the specific thing you're proposing — not an implication the other person has to interpret.

"I liked that you mentioned urban beekeeping — want to get coffee sometime?" survives being read literally.

Six hundred words of hedging doesn't.

<CheckIn question="Which reads more clearly: stating your interest directly, or hedging it inside six hundred words?" />
