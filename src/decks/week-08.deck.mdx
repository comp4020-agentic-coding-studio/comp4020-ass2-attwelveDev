## Definitions

- **Apology** (noun) — a statement acknowledging fault, distinct from a statement acknowledging that the other person has feelings.
- **Group chat** (noun) — a shared messaging thread among several people, notable for periods of total silence followed by sudden, simultaneous activity.
- **Listening to someone** (verb) — to give conscious attention to someone speaking, not while thinking about video games or your coding project.

---

## Key point 1 — Actually listening, not waiting to talk

Put the phone face-down. Stop composing your reply while the other person is still speaking.

Before you respond, reflect back one thing they actually said — "so you're saying X" — then add your own point.

If you can't repeat back what they just said, you weren't listening, you were waiting for your turn.

<CheckIn question="Could you repeat back the last full sentence your neighbour said before this lecture started?" />

## Key point 2 — The six components of an effective apology

Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., & Lount, R. B. (2016). *An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.* Negotiation and Conflict Management Research, 9(2), 177–196.

Expression of regret. Explanation. Acknowledgment of responsibility. Declaration of repentance. Offer of repair. Request for forgiveness.

<CheckIn question="Which component does 'I'm sorry you felt that way, but—' completely skip?" />

---

## Key point 3 — Which components matter most

Acknowledgment of responsibility mattered most.

Then offer of repair.

Request for forgiveness mattered least.

<CheckIn question="If you only had time for one component, which one does the research say to keep?" />

---

## Key point 4 — All six, assembled

"I should have told you the plan changed instead of letting you find out from someone else." — acknowledgment of responsibility.

"I got caught up finalising the venue and didn't think to message you until it was already too late." — explanation.

"I'm sorry — that's on me, not a good excuse." — expression of regret.

"I'll message you directly next time, not just the group chat." — declaration of repentance.

"I'd like to shout you coffee before the next one." — offer of repair.

No request for forgiveness — it mattered least, and this version doesn't need one.

<CheckIn question="Which of the six components is missing from this apology on purpose, and why does the study say that's fine?" />

## Key point 5 — The incident-report format

What was agreed, what actually happened, and the actual reason.

Traced to a process failure — a message routed to the wrong chat, a step nobody owned — rather than to any one person's character.

The same no-blame reading this course applies everywhere else.

<CheckIn question="Why does the format ask for a process failure specifically, instead of just asking 'whose fault was it'?" />

## In-lecture activity

Split the room in half.

One half drafts a bad apology, missing acknowledgment of responsibility on purpose. The other drafts a fix.

Read two or three aloud.

## Conclusion

A good apology has real, identifiable components. Some matter more than others.

Turns "be a better friend" into a checklist.

You can now tell the difference between listening and waiting for your turn, assemble a real apology, and write up a mix-up as an incident report that traces the actual reason instead of assigning blame.
